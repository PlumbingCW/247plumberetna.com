What a dripping tub faucet actually is

A bathtub faucet drip is water slipping past a worn seal inside the valve, most often a cartridge, a rubber washer sitting on a brass seat, or the diverter that sends water up to the shower. The seal wears, the valve no longer closes fully, and a few drops a minute escape from the spout or the showerhead.

Think of the handle as a switch and the seal as the part that actually stops the water. Turning the handle only moves the seal out of the way. Over years of hot and cold cycles the rubber hardens, the plastic scores, and minerals pack into the gap.

A drip looks small, but a stream the width of a pencil lead can waste thousands of gallons over a year, and it stains the porcelain along the same line every day. The good news is that most drips come from one inexpensive part. The harder question is which part, and the sections below show how to tell.

Cartridge, washer or diverter: how they differ

A cartridge is a cylindrical plastic or brass insert that slides or rotates inside the valve body. It controls flow and mixing in one piece, so when it fails the whole cartridge is replaced. Single-handle tubs and many newer two-handle tubs use them.

A compression valve uses a rubber washer pressed down on a brass seat by a threaded stem. The washer wears flat and the seat can develop a rough edge, which is why a new washer sometimes fixes the drip for a month and then it returns. Older two-handle and three-handle tubs often work this way.

The diverter is the third suspect. On a tub spout it is a pull-up knob on the top of the spout, or an internal flap, that redirects water to the shower arm. When it fails, water keeps coming out of the showerhead after the tub fills, or the shower is weak and the spout keeps running.

Tub spout leak or shower arm leak? Read the water

Where the drip appears points to the part. Run the tub for a minute, then watch carefully when you shut it off.

Drips from the spout only

The valve is passing water. Look at the cartridge or washer first. A diverter that lifts and seals fully will not cause this.

Drips from the showerhead only

The diverter is not seating, or the cartridge leaks and water is finding the shower path. Check the diverter first because it is the cheaper part.

Water at the spout wall joint

The spout connection is the leak, not the valve. A loose or cracked spout lets water run down the wall instead of out the spout.

Water at the shower arm wall joint

The arm threads into a fitting behind the wall. A weeping joint there needs re-sealing with the arm unscrewed and fresh thread tape or paste.

Drip at the handle

Packing or O-ring leak, usually worse while the water is running. It rarely appears when the valve is off.

Stain on the ceiling below

A leak inside the wall. Stop using the tub and have the line checked, because the cause is not the handle trim.

How to replace a cartridge or washer

This is the common homeowner repair for a tub with a visible handle and no access panel trouble. Stop if anything feels corroded, round-edged or stuck, and call.

  1. 1

    Shut off the water

    Close the fixture shutoff if the tub has one, or the home's main shutoff. Open the tub tap to drain the line and confirm the water has stopped before touching the valve.

  2. 2

    Plug the drain and protect the tub

    Drop a rag in the drain so a small screw cannot fall in. Lay a towel on the tub floor so a dropped tool does not chip the finish.

  3. 3

    Remove the handle and trim

    Pry off the cap, take out the handle screw, then unscrew the escutcheon plate. Photograph each step so you can put it back in the same order.

  4. 4

    Pull the cartridge or stem

    A cartridge usually has a retaining clip or nut and pulls straight out, sometimes with a puller tool. A stem on a compression valve unscrews with a deep socket.

  5. 5

    Match the part exactly

    Bring the old cartridge or washer to the supplier. A close match is not a match, because stems differ in length and spline count.

  6. 6

    Reassemble and test

    Grease the O-rings with plumber's silicone, seat the new part fully, and restore the water slowly. Check for drips at the handle and the spout for several minutes.

Parts and tools to have on the bench

You can do most tub faucet work with a short list. A screwdriver set with both Phillips and flat heads, an adjustable wrench, a cartridge puller or large channel locks, plumber's grease, thread seal tape, a small flashlight and a handful of towels will cover it.

The part itself is the hard purchase. A cartridge is brand and model specific, and a universal one rarely fits. If you do not know the maker, look for a name stamped on the escutcheon or the handle, or take the old cartridge in a bag so the counter staff can compare ends and lengths.

Avoid plumber's putty or silicone on threaded water connections, because they can fail and they clog the valve. Use thread tape on tapered pipe threads and the right gasket or O-ring on seals. Replace the stem O-rings at the same time as the cartridge, since they cost almost nothing and they wear together.

DIY or pro: where the line usually sits

The repair itself is manageable for many homeowners. Judgment about what you find behind the trim is where experience pays off.

Reasonable to try

  • Replacing a single cartridge on a valve that shuts off cleanly
  • Swapping a diverter spout that screws on
  • Replacing handle O-rings or a visible packing seal
  • Re-caulking a tub spout joint after tightening it

Call a plumber

  • The shutoff valves do not hold, so the water cannot be turned off at the tub
  • The valve body is loose, cracked or corroded behind the wall
  • Water is staining the wall or ceiling below the tub
  • The drip returns after a correct new cartridge, which points to the seat or the body

When the valve body is the problem

The valve body is the brass or plastic housing in the wall that holds the cartridge. Cartridges are meant to be replaced, but the body is part of the plumbing. If the seating surface inside is scored or the body is cracked, a new cartridge cannot seal against it.

Warning signs include a new cartridge that leaks within days, mineral crust that will not clean out of the cavity, a body that turns when you remove the retaining nut, or green and white corrosion on the brass. Any of these means the next step is replacing the valve, which often needs wall access from behind or a cut in the tile.

That is a bigger repair, and it is the point to stop trying one more cartridge. When the leak is behind the wall, our faucet and valve repair work covers valve replacement, and our leak detection methods help find a slow wall leak before it ruins drywall.

Etna homes and the age of their tub valves

The Census Bureau's 2024 five-year estimate puts the median year built for homes in Etna Township at 1995. The 2023 estimate counts about 2,030 homes from the 1990s and about 1,999 from the 2000s. A tub valve installed in the mid-1990s is now roughly 30 years old, about the age when rubber seals and plastic cartridges give out.

If your home falls in that range, a drip is more likely to be ordinary wear than a defect. It is reasonable to replace the cartridge and its O-rings together, and to check the shutoffs while you have the water off.

For much of the township, tap water is supplied by LRWD, and its 2025 water quality report says its plant removes calcium and magnesium by nano-filtration. We cannot tell you the mineral content of your tap from here, so for a fixture that scales up fast, test your tap or ask the district.

Common mistakes that make a drip worse

The most frequent error is overtightening. Cranking the handle shut to stop a drip compresses the washer and scores the seat, so the next drip comes sooner. If the handle needs real force to stop the water, the part is worn and should be replaced.

Another is working without a confirmed shutoff. A tub valve with the cartridge removed is an open pipe at full pressure, usually somewhere between 40 and 80 psi, and a failed fixture stop turns a small repair into a wet bathroom. Test the shutoff by running the tap, and if water keeps coming, stop and call.

Last, do not ignore the second symptom. A drip that is hot only, or a shower that stays warm after you turn it off, can mean a hot-side leak. If you cannot stop it, or you see damp drywall, our team is reachable at any hour, and we serve Etna alongside neighbors in Pataskala and Reynoldsburg.
