Is it condensation, seepage or a plumbing leak?

Basement waterproofing is the set of drainage and sealing measures that keep groundwater and surface water out of a basement. The right one depends on where the moisture comes from, which is why we figure that out before anything else.

What you seeLikely causeHow to tell
Damp patches on cold walls in summer, wet cold pipesCondensation from humid airWorse in warm, muggy weather and clears with a dehumidifier
Water at the wall-floor joint after rainGroundwater or surface runoffFollows rainfall by hours or a day, may smell earthy
Wet spot that appears with no rainPlumbing leak, supply or drainThe meter moves, or the spot tracks with water use
White powder on masonryMineral salts left behind by moving moistureBrushes off, returns where moisture keeps coming through

A simple test: tape a square of plastic or foil to the wall for a day or two. Moisture on the room side points to condensation, and moisture under the plastic points to seepage.

Trace the moisture before you pay for anything

Waterproofing work can cost a lot of money, so the first job is figuring out what is actually wet. A dehumidifier, a downspout extension or a pipe repair can settle many cases for far less than interior drainage.

We check for plumbing leaks first with a meter test and a look at supply lines, drains and the water heater. We also check the sump, the floor drain and any laundry connections. If the source is plumbing, leak detection finds it. If the source is groundwater, we move on to the outside.

Outside the house: the cheapest fixes come first

Sending roof water farther from the foundation costs the least and is checked first.

  • ✓Downspouts that discharge at least several feet from the wall, with extensions or splash blocks
  • ✓Gutters that are clean and not overflowing at the corners
  • ✓Soil that slopes away from the foundation, not toward it
  • ✓Window wells that are clear, with a drain at the bottom if possible
  • ✓Planting beds and mulch kept below the siding and not building up against it
  • ✓Sump pump discharge ending well away from the house

When interior drainage makes sense

If outside fixes are in place and water still enters, interior drainage manages it where it arrives.

  1. 1

    Open a channel

    A trench is cut along the inside edge of the slab, along the wall where water enters.

  2. 2

    Lay drain tile

    A perforated pipe set in gravel collects water at the footing.

  3. 3

    Route to a sump

    The pipe slopes to a sump pit, and a pump sends the water outdoors. See sump pump installation and repair.

  4. 4

    Close and finish

    New concrete patches the trench. The wall surface is left for you or a contractor to finish.

  5. 5

    Test with water

    We run water through the system to confirm that it flows to the pit and the pump clears it.

Wall cracks, bowing walls and where plumbing work stops

Hairline cracks in poured concrete are common and often cosmetic, but a crack that is wider than a pencil line, stair-steps through block, or leaks after every rain should be looked at. A wall that bows inward, or a floor that heaves, points to soil pressure and structure, not drainage.

That is outside plumbing. A structural engineer should evaluate it, and the drainage work we do may follow the engineer's plan. We do the plumbing and drainage side: drain tile, sump pits, pumps, discharge lines and floor drains. We do not promise a dry basement, and we do not offer a warranty on seepage. We tell you what we expect the work to do.

Surface fixes or interior drain tile

Surface fixes first

  • Downspout extensions, grading and gutter cleaning cost little and take little time
  • They reduce the water against the foundation in the first place
  • They cannot handle a high water table or a wall that stays wet
  • They are worth trying before any interior excavation

Interior drain tile

  • Collects water that gets to the footing and gets it out
  • Works in all seasons and does not depend on yard slope
  • Requires cutting the slab and running a sump pump
  • Does not stop water from entering the wall, it only controls it

What affects the work

Each basement has its own conditions. We look at these before recommending drainage.

Soil type

Soil that drains slowly holds water against the wall, so seepage is more likely.

Slope of the yard

Yards that slope toward the house push runoff to the foundation.

Foundation type

Poured concrete, block and old stone behave differently when wet.

Floor finish

Carpet, tile or a finished wall will need removal in the drain path.
