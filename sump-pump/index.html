Sump pump failed during a storm

A pit filling with water and a silent pump is an emergency, because a basement can start taking water within hours of a heavy rain. A live person picks up 24/7 in Etna. Do not wait for the water to reach the floor, because lifting boxes and stored items off the slab now is the cheapest protection you have.

A sump pump is a small electric pump set in a pit below the basement floor. It moves groundwater out and away from the house before it can rise onto the slab.

If the pump is humming but not moving water, or the pit is rising, call us. Keep your hands out of the pit, and unplug the pump only if the floor around the outlet is dry. If the power is out, the pump cannot run, and a battery backup is the only thing that will.

Check the power

Make sure the pump is plugged into a working outlet and the GFCI has not tripped. A tripped breaker is a common cause.

Look at the float

Lift the float by hand if you can reach it safely. A pump that starts when lifted points to a stuck float or tangled switch.

Move belongings up

Get electronics, boxes and furniture off the lowest floor, and keep the water heater and furnace area clear.

How sump pumps fail

Most failures are mechanical and slow, and they show up during the first big rain after a dry spell.

Stuck float switch

The float rises with the water to start the pump. If it wedges against the pit wall or its tether tangles, the pump never starts. It is a common failure.

Burned-out motor

A pump that runs constantly, or cycles on and off every few seconds, may be at the end of its life. A smell of hot plastic is another warning.

Failed check valve

The valve in the discharge pipe stops water from flowing back into the pit. If it fails, the pump short-cycles, and you may hear a thump when it shuts off.

Clogged intake or frozen discharge

Silt can block the intake, and a discharge line that freezes outdoors can trap the water in the pipe.

Test your sump pump by pouring in water

Do this in spring and again in fall, and any time before a forecast storm.

  1. 1

    Look at the pit

    Check that the lid is on, the pit is free of debris and the pump sits upright.

  2. 2

    Pour in water

    Fill the pit with a few buckets until the float rises. A healthy pump starts, empties the pit, then shuts off.

  3. 3

    Watch the discharge

    Go outside while it runs and make sure water leaves the discharge pipe and drains away from the house.

  4. 4

    Listen for trouble

    Grinding, rattling or a pump that runs after the pit is empty should be checked.

  5. 5

    Test the backup

    If you have a battery backup, unplug the main pump and repeat. Check the battery's age and charge.

Backup pump options compared

A backup matters most when the power goes out in the same storm that raises the water table.

Backup typeHow it worksTrade-offs
Battery backup pumpA second pump runs from a deep-cycle battery when the main pump or power failsBattery needs checking and replacing over time, and run time is limited
Water-powered backupUses household water pressure to push pit water out through a jetNeeds pressurized municipal water, and is generally a poor fit for a home on a private well
Second AC pumpA second mains-powered pump in the same pit, with its own floatCovers pump failure but not a power outage
Generator circuitRuns the pump from a whole-house or portable generatorDepends on someone being home and the generator working

We recommend a backup based on how often your pit runs and how much water arrives in a storm.

Check valve, discharge line and pit size

The check valve sits on the discharge pipe just above the pump, and the arrow on its body shows the direction of flow. The discharge line should carry water outdoors and away from the foundation. Do not connect it to the house sewer drain, and check what local rules say about where the water may go.

A pit that is too small makes a pump start and stop constantly, which wears it out. The right pit size depends on how much water arrives, and a deeper, wider pit gives the pump more room to work. A larger pit gives the pump longer runs and cooler motor temperatures.

Ejector pumps for basement bathrooms

A sump pump handles groundwater. An ejector pump is a different machine that lifts wastewater from a basement toilet, shower or laundry up to the sewer line, because those fixtures sit below it. The pit is sealed, and the pump handles solids.

If the basin alarm sounds, the pump has failed or the float is stuck. Stop using the fixtures that drain to it, and call us. Signs of trouble include slow drains in the basement bath or a sewer smell near the lid. For a problem on the house sewer line instead, see sewer line cleaning.

Repair the pump or replace it

Many pumps can be fixed, but there is a point where a repair is a waste.

Repair makes sense when

  • The pump is under a few years old and failed from a stuck float or clogged intake
  • The check valve or discharge pipe is the culprit
  • The motor runs quietly and the impeller turns freely

Replace when

  • The motor is burned out or the housing is cracked
  • The pump is rusted or has run through many storm seasons
  • The unit is too small for the pit's inflow or short-cycles constantly
