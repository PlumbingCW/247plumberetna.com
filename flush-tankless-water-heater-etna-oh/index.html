Do You Need to Flush a Tankless Water Heater in Etna, OH?

Mineral scale builds up inside the heat exchanger, and a flush is how it comes out.

Scale and error codesVinegar or descaler flushTest your tap first

By the Etna 24/7 Plumbing team

Yes, in general you do need to flush a tankless water heater. A flush means circulating a descaling solution, usually white vinegar or a descaler made for the purpose, through the heat exchanger to dissolve the mineral scale that heated water leaves behind. Skipping it will not wreck the unit overnight, but scale thickens slowly and the heater has to work harder to do the same job.

This guide covers why scale forms, what the warning signs look like, how a flush works at a high level, and how often to do one in Etna, OH. It also covers what a flush cannot fix and when a plumber should take over. Your unit's manual always outranks a general article, so read it before you touch a valve.

Why scale forms inside a tankless heater

Water carries dissolved calcium and magnesium. When a tankless unit heats that water in seconds, some of those minerals leave the solution and cling to the hottest metal surface, which is the heat exchanger. The coating starts as a film and grows with every hot-water draw.

Scale acts like insulation. An exchanger wrapped in a mineral crust needs more fuel or more electricity to reach the set temperature, and the narrow water passages inside it start to restrict flow. A scaled unit can deliver lukewarm water, a weak stream or a safety shutdown even though no part has failed.

A tank heater hides the same problem because sediment drops to the bottom of the tank. A tankless heater has no storage, so the minerals stay in the channels where the water is heated. Tight passages and fast heating are exactly what make this style of heater sensitive to scale.

Signs your tankless heater is asking for a flush

None of these proves scale by itself, but a cluster of them points that way. Rule out the simple things first: gas supply open, power on, setpoint where you left it.

Lukewarm or swinging temperature

Water that never reaches the setpoint, or that surges hot and cold during a shower, can mean the exchanger is no longer passing heat well.

Weak flow at hot taps only

If cold water is strong at a faucet and hot is weak, scale or a clogged inlet screen may be restricting the unit. Test a second faucet to rule out a fixture problem.

Error codes on the display

Most units show a numeric code when they sense overheating, poor flow or poor heat transfer. Codes differ by maker, so look yours up in the manual. The same code can have more than one cause.

Rumbling or popping sounds

Kettle-like noises as the water heats can mean scale is trapping small pockets of water against hot metal.

What to gather before you start

A flush goes smoothly when everything is within reach before the first valve moves.

  • ✓The manufacturer's manual, open to the maintenance page
  • ✓A small submersible pump and two washing-machine style hoses with fittings that suit your service ports
  • ✓A five-gallon bucket for the solution and a second container for rinse water
  • ✓White vinegar or the descaler the manufacturer recommends
  • ✓Gloves and eye protection, because hot water and acid both cause injuries
  • ✓A hardness test kit so you can write down what your tap actually delivers

How a descaling flush works, step by step

This is the outline, not a replacement for the manual. Water inside the unit can scald, so let it cool before anything else.

  1. 1

    Shut the unit down

    Switch off the power, close the gas valve if the unit burns gas, and wait for the exchanger to cool. A gas-fired heater should never be worked on with the flame lit.

  2. 2

    Close the isolation valves

    Service valves on the hot and cold lines let you seal the unit off from the house. Without them the flush is impractical, and a plumber can add a pair so every later flush takes minutes of setup.

  3. 3

    Connect the hoses

    One hose runs from the pump to the cold-side service port. The other runs from the hot-side port back into the bucket, forming a closed loop. Open the service port caps so the solution can move through.

  4. 4

    Circulate the solution

    Pour in enough undiluted vinegar to cover the pump, or the descaler dose the maker lists, and run the loop for the time in your manual. Open a window, because vinegar fumes are sharp. Never mix vinegar with bleach or another cleaner.

  5. 5

    Rinse and clean the screen

    Drain the solution, flush the unit with clean water, then pull the inlet filter screen and rinse out any grit or flakes. Scale on that screen tells you how aggressive your water is.

  6. 6

    Return to service

    Close the service ports, open the isolation valves slowly, run a hot tap to purge air, then restore gas and power. Look at every connection for drips before you walk away.

How often: test your tap first

There is no single schedule that fits every house. Many manufacturers suggest about once a year, homes with heavier scale benefit from more frequent flushes, and homes with very little dissolved mineral can stretch the interval. The deciding factor is your water, not the calendar.

Collect a sample from an indoor tap, run a hardness kit or send a sample to a lab, and ask your water supplier what it publishes. Annual is a sensible default if the result is moderate or high. Sooner is better if you see symptoms.

Write down the date and any error code at each flush. After two visits you will see what came out: cloudy liquid, white flakes or gritty sediment. That record lets you tighten or relax the schedule with evidence instead of guessing.

What the Etna-area water report does and does not tell you

Homes on the Licking Regional Water District system get groundwater from six wells beside the district's treatment plant on York Road. The district's 2025 water-quality report describes a nano-filtration step to remove calcium and magnesium, the two minerals behind scale. That is useful context for a tankless owner, but a treatment step is not the same thing as a measured result at your faucet.

The report gives no hardness figure in milligrams per liter or grains per gallon, and we will not guess one for you. So test an indoor tap yourself, then judge the first flush by what the inlet screen and the used solution look like. If both come out clean after a year, you have good evidence that your interval can stretch.

Private wells fall outside that report, and well water can differ from house to house even on the same road. The Licking County Health Department regulates private water systems, so well owners with testing questions can start there. A well home with a tankless heater should test before setting any schedule.

What a flush does not fix

A flush removes scale and clears the inlet screen. It does not repair a failed igniter, flame sensor, fan, flow sensor or temperature sensor, and it cannot patch a cracked heat exchanger. If a code returns right after a thorough flush, the cause is something else.

It also cannot undo years of neglect in one visit. A heavily scaled exchanger may need repeat circulation or the maker's own descaling procedure, and in the worst case the passages are blocked beyond recovery. A flush is maintenance, not prevention.

Prevention means dealing with the minerals before they reach the heater. If testing shows a lot of dissolved mineral, a water softener or filtration system is the long-term fix, and a flush then becomes a lighter chore.

Homeowner flush or plumber visit?

Plenty of owners handle a flush safely. The line is drawn by the valves you have, the fuel the unit burns and how it is behaving.

Reasonable to try yourself

  • The unit already has isolation valves and service ports
  • It runs normally and you are doing routine upkeep
  • You have the manual and the right pump and hoses
  • You can keep the space ventilated and avoid scalds

Better with a plumber

  • There are no isolation valves, or they leak or will not turn
  • An error code remains after a flush or returns quickly
  • You smell gas, see soot or hear a carbon monoxide alarm
  • The unit leaks, or the vent pipe looks loose or damaged

When to call a plumber

Gas smell, a carbon monoxide alarm, scorch marks or a flooded floor all mean the same thing: stop, ventilate and call the gas utility or 911 first. A flush is the wrong response to any of them. Once the area is safe, a person can take the call about the repair.

For everything short of that, our tankless water heater service covers flushing, isolation valve installs, diagnosis and repair, and a water heater repair visit can sort out a unit that still misbehaves after a descale.
