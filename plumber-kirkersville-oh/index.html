Water everywhere or a drain backing up in Kirkersville? Start here

Kirkersville homes sit on three different water setups, so the first minute of an emergency depends on which one you have. Our emergency dispatch answers around the clock, and the steps below are safe to do while you dial.

If you are on a private well, the water stops at a pressure tank and pump instead of a city main. If you are on the village sewer, a backup means the problem may be in your house line or farther down the street.

Find the right shutoff

On a well, switch off the pump breaker so the pressure tank stops feeding a leak. On public water, close the main valve near the meter or where the line enters the house.

Stop adding water

Hold off on laundry, dishwashers and showers when a drain is slow or gurgling. Every gallon you add has to go somewhere.

Check for a grinder pump alarm

If your property has a grinder pump, a panel alarm means stop using water and call right away. Do not silence it and carry on.

Keep power and water apart

Stay out of a wet basement if water is near the panel, outlets or a sump pump cord, and tell us when you call.

Three water systems in one small village

Kirkersville is a village of a few hundred people in Harrison Township, with a slice reaching into Etna Township. The village owns its sewer and runs its own wastewater plant. Most homes still draw water from private wells, and the Licking Regional Water District has been extending public water lines into the village.

Village council minutes from April 2026 describe about 75 to 80 properties already connected. That matters to you because each hookup needs a customer-side service line and meter setting, and the district has said that a home keeping its well for outdoor watering needs a backflow device fitted by a certified plumber. Ask the district what applies to your address, then call us for the plumbing side.

Older homes along the National Road

The village grew as a pike town on US-40, and Census estimates put the median home here at about 1953, with a wide margin because the village is small. About three in five homes predate 1960. Houses from that period often carry galvanized supply pipe and cast-iron or clay drains.

In practice, that means rusty or weak water at the tap, repeat clogs and old water heaters. If your pipes are that old, a repiping visit or a camera look through sewer inspection shows what you are really dealing with before you spend money on patches.

Rain, the South Fork and wet basements

The South Fork Licking River runs through and near the village. The National Weather Service lists crests at its Kirkersville gauge that include about 14 feet in March 2020 and about 10.4 feet in April 2024. The village keeps its own flood hazard permit rules, and one neighborhood association has said some properties near Route 40 carry flood insurance.

For you, the lesson is simple. Test your sump pump before the spring thaw by pouring water into the pit and watching the float lift. If the pump hesitates, call before the storm, not during it. Our basement waterproofing page explains how we separate groundwater from a plumbing leak.

Permits and who to ask

A zoning permit from the village comes first on most building jobs, and building permits themselves run through the Licking County Building Code Department. Plumbing permits for new residential and commercial work go through the Licking County Health Department plumbing program, which also requires plumbers to register.

Not every repair needs a permit. When yours does, we will say so before work begins. If you want to check on your own, the village zoning inspector and the county health department are the two offices to call.
