What a planned installation covers

Water heater installation means setting a new unit where none stood before, or moving and upgrading one, and connecting water, fuel or power, venting, safety valves and a drain path. It differs from a same-day swap because there is time to choose the size, the location and the fuel on purpose. If your tank has already failed and is leaking, water heater replacement is the faster path.

Most planned jobs begin with plain questions. How many people live in the house, how many bathrooms run at once, and is there a soaking tub or a second kitchen? Those answers matter more than the old unit's size, which may have been wrong from the start.

Sizing by first-hour rating, not just gallons

Tank gallons are only half the picture. The first-hour rating states how much hot water a unit can supply in one busy hour starting from a full, hot tank, and it is printed on the yellow EnergyGuide label.

Count the peak hour

List the showers, the dishwasher and the laundry that overlap on a normal morning. Two people with one bathroom peak very differently from a family of five.

Match the rating to the peak

Pick a unit whose first-hour rating covers that peak with a little room to spare. Oversizing wastes standby heat, and undersizing leaves the last person in line with cold water.

Mind recovery speed

Gas tanks reheat faster than standard electric ones, so a smaller gas tank can match a larger electric tank on first-hour delivery.

Check the space

Tall slim tanks, lowboys and wall-hung units fit where a standard round tank will not. Measure ceiling height and the room around the flue before you shop.

How a planned installation is sequenced

  1. 1

    Walk the site

    We look at where the unit will sit, the gas or electric supply, the vent route and the nearest floor drain for the pan and relief valve discharge.

  2. 2

    Settle scope and the permit question

    Some installs need a permit and an inspection and others do not, and rules vary by jurisdiction. Your township or county building office has the final word, and we will tell you which kind of job yours looks like.

  3. 3

    Rough in the supply

    A new gas branch, a dedicated circuit, water lines and the vent run go in first. A new 240-volt circuit may need an electrician, and we say so up front.

  4. 4

    Set and connect

    The tank goes on a pan with new connectors, a relief valve and discharge pipe, and an expansion tank where the system is closed.

  5. 5

    Test and walk through

    We fire or energize the unit, check temperature at a tap, inspect every joint, then show you the cold shutoff, the gas control or breaker, and the drain valve.

Four common reasons to plan an install

New construction

A builder may rough in pipe, but the heater type, size and location are still open. Settling them early avoids a tank squeezed into a closet that cannot vent it.

Remodels

A new bathroom or kitchen adds fixtures, and the old heater may no longer keep up. We check the first-hour rating against the new peak before walls close.

Fuel switches

Moving off an old gas line or onto a heat pump unit needs a new circuit or capped piping. Planning it avoids a rushed change after a failure.

Relocation

Heaters get moved out of cramped utility closets or away from freezing garages. Moving one means new pipe runs and a fresh look at venting.

Permits and code, plainly

Work is done to the Ohio Plumbing Code. Whether your job needs a permit depends on the scope and on the jurisdiction, so check with the building office for your township or county. A new gas line, a fuel switch and a relocation are the kinds of work that more often draw a permit question, and we will tell you which applies. If you are buying or selling a home, settling the question before the install avoids surprises later.

Etna Township homes and the age of the heater

About 4,000 of the township's 7,100 occupied homes were built between 1990 and 2009, and the median year built is 1995. Only about 290 predate 1940, so this is not an old housing stock. The 1970s are the next biggest group, at about 1,200 homes. A heater that came with a 1990s build is long past its expected life, which makes a planned install on your schedule far better than a forced one.

Licking County Health Department requires plumbers to be registered and issues permits for new plumbing work, with rough-in and final inspections. A brand new installation is new work, so expect that process and ask us which rules apply before the first pipe is cut. Houses near Kirkersville, Pataskala and Reynoldsburg are served the same way.
