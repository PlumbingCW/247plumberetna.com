How to Install a Garbage Disposal in Etna, OH

A step-by-step walkthrough for swapping or adding a sink disposal, including the electrical truth and the points where you should stop.

What installing a garbage disposal involves

Installing a garbage disposal means mounting a food-waste grinder to the sink's drain opening, connecting its outlet to the trap, supplying power, and proving every joint is dry. The unit replaces the basket strainer at the drain, so the sink flange, the mounting ring, and the discharge elbow all have to line up before anything gets fully tightened.

If you have swapped a faucet or a P-trap before, the plumbing half of this job is within reach. The electrical half is where people get hurt, and the cabinet is cramped enough that a helper with a headlamp is worth having. Read the whole sequence first, then decide whether this is a job for you or a call.

Gather these before you shut anything off

Everything on the counter first means the sink is out of service for as little time as possible.

  • ✓A disposal with a horsepower rating that suits the household, plus the mounting kit in the box
  • ✓Plumber's putty or the gasket that ships with the new sink flange, and a screwdriver set
  • ✓Channel-lock pliers, a hammer, and a flat screwdriver for the dishwasher knockout plug
  • ✓A new discharge gasket, and a trap arm or tailpiece extension if the old one is the wrong length
  • ✓A non-contact voltage tester and a headlamp, because the cabinet is dark
  • ✓A bucket, old towels, and a shop vacuum for the water that always waits in the trap
  • ✓A power cord kit if the unit ships without one, or a cable clamp and wire connectors if you will hardwire

Install it in this order

Work through these in sequence and do not skip ahead, because the first step protects you through all the rest.

  1. 1

    Kill the power and lock it out

    Turn off the breaker that feeds the disposal circuit, then tape over it or label it so nobody flips it back on. Confirm the outlet or junction box is dead with a non-contact voltage tester, and prove the tester works on a known live outlet first. If you cannot say which breaker feeds the sink, stop and find out before touching anything else.

  2. 2

    Remove the old strainer or old disposal

    Set a bucket under the trap and loosen the slip nuts. An old disposal comes off by turning its lower mounting ring with a screwdriver in the lugs while you support its weight. Scrape the old putty off the sink opening so the new flange can seat flat.

  3. 3

    Seat the sink flange and mounting ring

    Roll a rope of plumber's putty under the new flange's lip, or use the gasket supplied, and press the flange into the drain opening from above. From below, slide on the fiber gasket, backup ring, and mounting ring, then tighten the three mounting screws evenly until putty squeezes out. Wipe the squeeze-out and stop at snug, because a cranked ring warps.

  4. 4

    Knock out the dishwasher plug

    If the dishwasher drains through the disposal, the side inlet has a solid plug inside it that must come out. Set a screwdriver against it, tap with a hammer until it drops into the grind chamber, then fish the plug out with pliers. If no dishwasher connects, leave the plug alone.

  5. 5

    Hang the unit

    Lift the disposal under the flange, line its upper ring up with the lugs on the mounting assembly, and twist until it locks. Turn the body so the discharge outlet points toward the trap arm, since the outlet will not move once the ring is tight. Have a helper hold the weight, because the unit is heavy and awkward from below.

  6. 6

    Connect the trap and the dishwasher hose

    Bolt the discharge elbow to the outlet with its gasket, then run the trap arm to the wall stub-out so it slopes gently toward the wall. The tailpiece and P-trap should meet without being pulled sideways, because forcing a misaligned joint tight is the usual source of a slow weep. Clamp the dishwasher hose to the side inlet, looping it high under the counter before it drops to the inlet.

  7. 7

    Connect the power

    Wire or plug in the unit using the guidance in the next section, with the breaker still off. Do not restore power yet, because the leak test comes first.

  8. 8

    Run the leak test

    Put dry paper towel under the sink, plug the drain, fill the basin partway, then release it so a full flow passes through the disposal and trap. Run a dry fingertip along every joint. Restore power, run cold water, switch the unit on for a few seconds, and check again while it runs, because vibration finds loose joints that standing water misses.

Cord or hardwire: the honest answer

Many disposals ship without a cord, so the choice depends on what is under your sink now. A cord kit plugged into a grounded outlet is the simpler route, and hardwiring means landing the black, white, and ground conductors in the unit's wiring compartment and finishing with a cable clamp that keeps the cable from tugging the connections. Manufacturer instructions differ, so follow the ones in your box.

If there is no existing outlet or switch, or the cabinet wiring looks old, damaged, or ungrounded, stop here. Adding a circuit or repairing wiring is electrician work, and a wet cabinet is a poor place to learn. Whether your house needs GFCI protection depends on its wiring and the local rules, so ask the building office instead of guessing.

Most problems after an install trace back to one of a handful of causes. Use this to decide whether to fix it yourself or stop.

Choosing the unit and checking the sink

Continuous-feed disposals run while you hold a wall switch on, and batch-feed models run only when a lid is twisted in place. Motor size is rated in horsepower, and a bigger motor handles tougher scraps but needs more cabinet room and costs more to run. Measure the space under the sink before you buy, including the room the discharge elbow needs.

A standard kitchen sink drain opening is about 3.5 inches across, and most disposals are made to fit it. A double-bowl sink needs the second bowl routed into the disposal's discharge or into the trap arm, so look at how the two bowls join before you start.

The Census Bureau's 2024 five-year estimate puts the median year a home in Etna Township was built at 1995, and about 4,000 of the township's roughly 7,000 occupied homes date from 1990 to 2009. Many kitchens here are therefore two to three decades old. Check the trap, tailpiece, and shutoff valves under your sink, and replace anything crusty or weeping before the disposal is mounted on top of it.

Licking Regional Water District provides centralized sewer service to part of Etna Township, according to its own site, and which addresses are connected varies street by street. Your utility bill will show whether you have a sewer charge, and the district can confirm for your address. Where your home drains matters because it decides what the disposal's grindings go into, which is why the first question for any kitchen install is where the drain line ends.

Do it yourself or hire it out

A swap of an existing disposal on existing wiring is a different job from a first-time install.

Reasonable to do yourself

  • Replacing a disposal in the same position with a matching cord and outlet
  • Reseating the flange and replacing gaskets
  • Knocking out the dishwasher plug
  • Replacing a trap arm or tailpiece with the same type of pipe

Better to hire out

  • No outlet, switch, or grounded wiring at the sink
  • A drain that slopes the wrong way or gurgles before you start
  • Rusted, seized, or leaking shutoff valves you cannot move, or a faucet that is also failing (see faucet repair)
  • Any smell of burning, signs of a past leak in the cabinet floor, or a cabinet that has soft, swollen wood
