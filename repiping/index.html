What repiping is, and when it beats patching

Repiping is the replacement of a home's water supply lines, the pipes that carry pressurized water to the faucets, showers and appliances, usually through most or all of the house. It is a larger project than repairing a leak, so it is worth doing only when patching has stopped making sense.

One leak in a sound system is a repair. Repeated leaks, rusty water at every tap and shrinking pressure across the house are signs that the pipe itself has aged out. A plumber can often repair pipe three times in a year and still not be ahead. We will tell you honestly when that point has arrived.

Signs your pipes are due for replacement

Brown or orange water

Rusty water on first use in the morning points to corroding galvanized steel or iron.

Low pressure at every tap

The inside of old galvanized pipe narrows as scale builds up, until a shower is a trickle.

Pinhole leaks in copper

Tiny green-edged pinholes, especially in more than one spot, mean the copper is thinning from within.

Repeated repairs

A second or third leak within a year usually means the next one is already forming.

Polybutylene pipe

Gray plastic pipe found in many homes built between roughly the late 1970s and the mid 1990s is known for failing at fittings.

Stained ceilings

Yellow or brown rings beneath bathrooms can come from a supply line weeping in the wall.

Choosing a replacement material

No pipe is perfect for every house. The options below are common choices in residential work.

MaterialStrengthsTrade-offs
PEXFlexible, fewer joints, can be pulled through walls with less demolitionMust be kept out of sunlight, and some people prefer metal
CopperLong track record, rigid, tolerates heat, no plasticMore labor and joints, and some water conditions attack it, so test your tap
CPVCLower material cost, handles hot water to a pointCan become brittle with age, and is less common for whole-house work

We recommend a material after looking at your walls, your water and what you want to spend.

How a whole-house repipe is sequenced

A repipe is planned so the water is off for as little time as possible.

  1. 1

    Walk-through and plan

    We trace the existing lines, note every fixture and appliance, and decide where new lines will run. The plan includes where walls, ceilings or floors need access.

  2. 2

    Rough-in

    New supply lines are run through accessible spaces first, such as attics, basements and crawlspaces, to limit wall openings.

  3. 3

    Wall access

    Small openings are cut where new lines must pass or connect, and each is kept as small as we can manage.

  4. 4

    Connect and test

    New lines are tied to the fixtures and the water is turned on to pressure-test the system for leaks before anything is closed.

  5. 5

    Old pipe out or capped

    Old lines inside walls are often capped and left, and removed when they are in the way.

  6. 6

    Patch and finish

    Drywall openings are patched to a paint-ready state. Painting and finish carpentry are separate trades.

Repair a section or repipe the house

The decision comes down to how much of the system is failing.

Section repair fits when

  • The leak is one failure in otherwise clean, sound pipe
  • The water is clear and pressure is steady at every fixture
  • The pipe is newer or made from a material without a known failure record

A repipe fits when

  • Leaks keep appearing in different places
  • Galvanized pipe is clogged with scale and rusty water is a regular problem
  • The house has polybutylene supply lines that you want to be rid of

Wall access and patching: what is honest to expect

Most repipes open some walls. Using PEX in attics and basements and fishing it through existing cavities reduces the number of openings, but fixtures on interior walls, two-story stacks and finished basements often need access holes. Plan for drywall patches and some painting.

We patch the openings we cut to a paint-ready condition, and we tell you before work starts where the holes will be. Match-painting, tile repair and finish carpentry are outside our scope. If you are remodeling soon, doing the repipe first saves you from opening the same wall twice.

What shapes the scope of a repipe

No two houses are alike. These are the factors that change how much work is involved.

House size and stories

More bathrooms, floors and fixtures mean more pipe and more connections.

Access

An open basement or crawlspace speeds the work, and finished ceilings slow it down.

Existing material

Pulling galvanized out of tight walls takes more effort than replacing copper.

Permits

Some jobs need a permit and an inspection. Rules vary by jurisdiction, so we contact the local building office and tell you what applies. Work is done to the Ohio Plumbing Code.

Before you commit: rule out a single hidden leak

A sudden pressure loss does not always mean the whole system is failing. A leaking service line, a bad pressure regulator or one burst section can mimic the same symptoms, and any of them is cheaper to fix than a repipe. We check these first with leak detection, and look at the buried line on the water service line repair page if that is where the trouble starts.
