Toilet overflowing or leaking right now?

An overflowing toilet can push water through a bathroom subfloor in a few minutes, so stop the flow before anything else. A person answers our line 24/7, and while you wait you can turn the chrome stop valve behind the bowl clockwise until it will not turn further. If the bowl keeps filling, lift the tank lid and press the rubber flapper down into its seat to cut off the tank.

Hold off on the plunger until the water level stops rising. Towels around the base buy time, and a wet vac clears a tiled floor faster than a mop. Whatever failed, the next step is working out whether the problem sits in the toilet itself or in the drain below it.

Stop the supply

Quarter turn or full turn clockwise on the stop valve, low on the wall or floor behind the bowl. Hand pressure only, since an old valve can snap.

Keep people off the floor

Water that has reached an outlet strip or a floor fan is an electrical hazard. Unplug from a dry spot or leave it and switch off the bathroom breaker.

Do not flush again

Each flush on a blocked line sends another 1.6 gallons or more into the bowl. Wait until someone has looked at the drain.

What your toilet is telling you

Most toilet calls fall into a handful of patterns. This table matches the symptom to the part that usually fails and the work that follows.

SymptomUsual causeWhat the repair involves
Water trickles into the bowl for minutes after a flushWorn flapper or a chain with too much slackNew flapper and chain adjustment, often under half an hour
Tank refills with a hiss, then refills again hours laterFill valve not shutting off or a leaking flapperReplace the fill valve, test with food coloring in the tank
Puddle at the base after every flushFailed wax ring or loose closet boltsPull the toilet, inspect the flange, set it on a new seal
Water beads on the outside of the tankCondensation on a cold tank in humid weatherUsually no repair, sometimes a tank liner or mixing valve
Weak flush, bowl rises, slowly drainsPartial clog or blocked rim jetsAuger the trap, clean the jets, check the vent if it recurs
Gurgling after flushing, other drains slowClog further down the lineTreat as a drain problem, see drain cleaning

A running or leaking toilet is a mechanical problem. A toilet that will not clear is usually a drain problem.

How we separate a clog from a failed part

The two look alike from the doorway, so the order of checks matters. Skipping straight to a plunger on a mechanical fault wastes time, and replacing parts on a blocked line fixes nothing.

  1. 1

    Check where the water goes

    Water that rises in the bowl and drains slowly points at a blockage. Water that never reaches the bowl, or that escapes outside it, points at a part.

  2. 2

    Test the tank

    Drop a few drops of food coloring into the tank and wait twenty minutes without flushing. Color in the bowl means the flapper or its seat is leaking.

  3. 3

    Try a closet auger

    A closet auger is built to pass the curved trap inside the bowl without scratching the porcelain. If the obstruction is a toy, a wipe or a wad of paper, it usually comes back on the first or second pass.

  4. 4

    Look further down the line

    If the auger runs free but the bowl still gurgles, we check a second fixture. Two slow fixtures at once mean the branch line, and that moves the job over to sewer line cleaning.

The running toilet that never quite stops

A toilet that runs on its own is the quietest way to waste water in a house. A worn flapper can leak a steady stream that nobody hears, and the tell is a water bill that creeps up while nothing else changed.

Most cures are small. The fill valve, the flapper, the chain and the flush lever are all replaceable in one visit, and a toilet more than a decade old often benefits from replacing all of them together rather than one at a time.

Seal, flange and bowl: what to leave to a plumber

Replacing a flapper is a fair weekend job. Lifting a toilet is different, because the bowl is heavy, the wax is messy and the flange underneath decides whether the new seal will hold.

Reasonable to try yourself

  • Closing the stop valve and checking whether it holds
  • Swapping a flapper that matches the tank brand and size
  • Tightening the supply line nut a quarter turn by hand and wrench
  • Using a plain flange plunger on a slow bowl

Better handled by a plumber

  • Rocking or leaking at the floor, which means a new seal and a flange check
  • Stains on the ceiling below the bathroom
  • A cracked tank or a hairline crack in the bowl
  • Repeat clogs, which point at the line rather than the toilet
  • A valve that spins without closing

Repair, or put in a new toilet?

Parts are cheap compared with a new bowl, but a repair that keeps coming back is no bargain. These are the points that tip the decision.

Age and cracks

A hairline crack in porcelain or a tank that weeps from its bolts cannot be sealed for good. The toilet itself is done.

Rough-in distance

Measure from the finished wall to the center of the floor bolts. Twelve inches is common, but ten and fourteen exist, and a wrong fit leaves a gap at the wall.

Bowl shape and height

Elongated bowls sit a few inches longer than round ones, and comfort-height models raise the seat. Check clearance to the vanity and door.

Flush design

Gravity-fed tanks are simple and cheap to fix later. Pressure-assist models flush hard but cost more to repair and run louder.

Installing a new toilet

We set the new bowl on a fresh seal, connect a new supply line, check the flange and confirm it does not rock. The old toilet leaves with us. If the flange or subfloor is damaged, we say so before we start.

Sewer, water and permits for a toilet job in Etna Township

Where public water and sewer serve a home in the township, they come from the Licking Regional Water District, so a bowl that gurgles with other drains slow is a question about your line, not the district's.

Replacing a toilet with the same or a similar one in the same spot falls under the exception in the Licking County plumbing rules, so no permit is needed for a straight swap. If the flange moves or new drain piping is involved, a permit and inspections apply, and we tell you which side your job lands on. Neighbors in Pataskala share the same water district.
